> **Precision statement.** The Execution Excellence System is HEE’s proposed framework for managing execution once intended work is not becoming reliable results: find what materially limits reliable execution, fix the condition that matters most, control the gain, learn and adapt, and strengthen future execution capability. It does not establish that its cycle improves execution, that its eight diagnostic domains are distinct and can be classified reliably, that its priority step can identify the constraint that matters most, or that execution management leads to sustainable business value. Those are questions for evidence.

> **Evidence status.** EES is a developing conceptual framework, not a validated method. Its cycle, diagnostic architecture, eight domains, four-part diagnostic lens, and maturity levels are proposals that require testing. Established work on systems thinking and quality management, organizational learning, constraint management, and strategy execution informs the framework; it does not validate EES’s particular integration or show that EES adds decision value beyond those methods. The author originated HEE and EES and has a commercial interest disclosed below. The AKG record is not an application of the EES cycle and does not test it. The references are a starting map, not a literature review. The [HEE Core README](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-economics) governs shared terminology where the documents differ.

**Evidence labels:** **[E]** existing research; **[A]** adaptation of existing ideas; **[P]** proposed and untested; **[H]** practice heuristic; **[Illustrative]** invented example. ## The question EES answers

An organization can have sound strategy, capable people, technology, resources, processes, information, and targets, and still execute unreliably. Priorities conflict, decisions are unclear, dependencies pile up, capability is fragmented, improvements do not hold, and the same failures recur. Leaders ask who should work harder. But execution can break down in several different places, and pushing harder on the wrong one consumes the capacity that future execution needs.

> **What prevents intended execution from becoming reliable execution, and what must change so that execution becomes stronger over time?**

In the Core’s vocabulary, an unreliable-execution signal is an **Execution Deficit**: a trigger for inquiry, not a cause. EES is the proposed structure for that inquiry and its follow-through. It is not a way to treat every performance problem as an execution problem; some are strategy, market, demand, or capability problems that belong elsewhere.

**Execution is a system that can be managed and improved, not a one-time event after strategy.** This is the premise and the proposed contribution, and both are open to testing. They are not findings.

## Owner view: one page

**[H] What to do with this document in ten minutes.**

1. **Name one execution problem** (a missed target, an unstable improvement, a recurring failure) and what it costs, if recorded. If nothing is recorded, that is the first finding.
2. **Say what should have happened and what happened,** for whom and over what period, without a cause.
3. **Ask which domain is involved:** demand, capability, connection, decision, enablement, flow, control, or learning. More than one usually is.
4. **Ask which constraint matters most,** not which is most visible, and what evidence supports that. Where the evidence does not discriminate, record Unknown and establish the cheapest evidence that would.
5. **Fix one thing first,** reversibly, with a stop rule and an owner. Keep any safeguard unless a replacement is shown, and name what takes over its protective purpose.
6. **Control only what keeps the gain,** in proportion, then review what execution taught you and what must change.
7. **Check tomorrow before celebrating today:** was the expenditure justified, and is credible next-cycle usable capacity sufficient for legitimate demand? Record Covered, Fragile, Not covered, or Unknown (see [Sustainability](#sustainability-capacity-and-value)).

**Speed [H].** Rigor scales with the stakes. An urgent, reversible decision can start with a first pass across the domains, a reversible first step, and a stop rule; a full study is not required. The [question map](#find-your-question) lists where else to look.

**Ask your team for:** the problem stated without a cause; the domains considered and the evidence for each; the priority and what was Unknown; the fix, its stop rule, and its owner; the controls and their cost; what was learned and what changed; the next-cycle capacity check.

**Do not:** treat activity as execution; fix the most visible problem by default; add control because a result slipped; call a result sustainable because it was achieved once.

What EES has and has not shown is in the [evidence status](#execution-excellence-system-ees) and the [AKG relationship](#relationship-to-the-akg-practice-record).

## The EES cycle

The cycle is **FIND → FIX → CONTROL → ADAPT → GROW**, and it repeats. It is the **canonical representation of the EES cycle in this document [H]**. The management flow, the diagnostic architecture, the case, and the coding sheet restate or apply it; they are not additional or competing models. The stages are an order of questions and activities, not a rigid procedure: a team may be at several stages for different problems at once, and may return to Find when a fix does not hold.

| Stage | Governing question | What it produces | What it must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **FIND** | What is preventing reliable execution now? | A supported account of what materially limits execution, with what is Unknown | A hunt for the most visible problem |
| **FIX** | What should be changed first because it matters most? | A condition-changing intervention, with a stop rule and an owner | A corrective action against a symptom |
| **CONTROL** | How do we stabilize the gain without adding burden? | Indicators, owners, thresholds, and reviews in proportion | Control for its own sake |
| **ADAPT** | What did execution teach us, and what should change, or deliberately stay, because of it? | A recorded decision to change or keep a method, decision, interface, control, or assumption | A report with no recorded decision |
| **GROW** | What must become stronger so the organization can execute better next time? | Strengthened capability, capacity, or readiness, checked against the next cycle | Assumed improvement |

### <a id="find"></a>Find

Find establishes what materially limits reliable execution, which is broader than identifying a visible problem. It distinguishes symptoms, performance gaps, constraints, capability gaps, connection gaps, decision problems, enablement conditions, flow problems, control weaknesses, and learning failures. It draws on the Core’s investigation routes: **Diagnose** to test candidate causes and **Establish** where evidence is missing. Find ends in a supported account, not a certainty: where the evidence does not discriminate, record Unknown, name the next evidence, and decide whether to wait or to act reversibly.

### <a id="fix"></a>Fix

Fix changes the condition that matters most. The most visible problem is not necessarily the most consequential one. A fix may remove a constraint, develop or connect capability, clarify a decision, change authority, improve conditions or interfaces, improve flow, change a method, strengthen or simplify a control, or address a recurring source of failure. Before changing a rule, approval, or control, test its purpose; use the constraint test in [HEEn](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-enablement#test-the-constraint-before-changing-it). Where the demand itself may be unnecessary, use [OSF](https://github.com/titumozammel/operational-silence-framework) and [5R](https://github.com/titumozammel/5r-cascade-framework). Compare feasible fixes by purpose served, cost, expected benefit, risk, reversibility, and effect on future capacity, and prefer a reversible first step with a stop rule written beforehand.

### <a id="control"></a>Control

Control stabilizes the improvement and protects the gain. It may include visibility, standards, thresholds, ownership, feedback, indicators, review, escalation, and corrective response. **Control is a stabilizing mechanism, not an objective in itself, and more control is not better execution.** Excessive control creates delay, dependency, intervention burden, operational noise, and reduced decision speed. Assess a control’s legitimate purpose, effectiveness, burden, alternatives, and applicable obligations, and preserve required protections (mandatory, safety-critical, or hard to quantify) while examining whether their design is proportionate. Useful controls are relevant, timely, informative, owned, and linked to action. Treat Control as a hypothesis too: record what it costs. The route **Verify** (comparing practice with a stated standard) belongs here.

### <a id="adapt"></a>Adapt

Adapt converts experience into changed understanding and changed action. **Correction** fixes a deviation; **adaptation** changes the system in response to learning or changing conditions, whether priorities, methods, capability, decisions, resources, interfaces, controls, processes, assumptions, or organizational design. Record what was learned separately from the decision to change, or deliberately keep, the system: a review can improve understanding or confirm an existing arrangement without requiring change, and “no change” does not automatically mean “no learning.” Count adaptation only where the system actually changed. Adaptation is not evidence of failure; assumptions, customers, technology, constraints, and strategy change. Reliable execution needs both consistency and adaptability, and control and adaptation are not opposites: control stabilizes the gain, and adaptation changes the system when learning or conditions require it.

### <a id="grow"></a>Grow

Grow strengthens future execution ability: capability, capacity, learning, adaptability, reliability, resilience, coordination, and decision quality. Grow is the least operationally defined stage, so this edition states its limits. It is not a separate intervention: it selects among the Core’s interventions (Protect, Recover, Develop, Connect, Enable) and routes to the frameworks that own them. It is verified by evidence, not assumed: use the Two Checks on the next cycle and look for results that hold under later conditions. **Execution leaves the organization better able to execute again** is the aspiration, and whether a given cycle achieves it is an empirical question. EES does not require every cycle to leave the organization stronger: necessary expenditure can be justified even when it temporarily reduces reserves.

## Diagnostic architecture

**[P] IECA → IECD → ECI.** EES proposes three diagnostic components. They describe the work of Find and its hand-off to Fix; they are not a competing operating model, and none replaces Find.

| Component | Plain-language label | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **IECA** — Integrated Execution Capability Assessment | Execution visibility | Establishes visibility across people, capabilities, processes, technology, decisions, coordination, resources, work flow, and execution conditions |
| **IECD** — Integrated Execution Capability Diagnostic | Execution diagnosis | Identifies the conditions preventing reliable execution |
| **ECI** — Execution Constraint Intelligence | Execution priority | Converts diagnosis into a judgment of which constraint, or combination, matters most to reliable execution |

**ECI is a judgment, not a formula.** It does not require, and this document does not offer, a universal numerical priority score. The shared heuristic *Priority = Urgency × Interdependence × Value Impact* applies as a heuristic, not as a validated equation or a meaningful number without defensible scales; use it alongside safety, authority, reversibility, uncertainty, and cost. Interdependence is not the 3C Dependency context. To limit rater bias, have each assessor record candidate constraints, the evidence for each, and what evidence would change the priority before any discussion. Whether independent assessors would pick the same constraint as the one that matters most is a test in the research agenda.

### <a id="domains"></a>Eight execution-constraint domains

**[P]** EES examines eight broad domains. They are **diagnostic domains, not mandatory stages**, and one problem may involve several. Treat them as a menu of inquiry, not a checklist to complete for every minor problem. A missed deadline may involve Decision, Connection, Flow, and Enablement together rather than being a time-management problem.

| Domain | Question | Typical signals (not diagnoses) | Where it may route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand | Is unnecessary or excessive demand entering the work? | Recurring requests, rework, avoidable coordination | Test necessity first (demand is not itself drain); then [OSF](https://github.com/titumozammel/operational-silence-framework) and [5R](https://github.com/titumozammel/5r-cascade-framework) |
| Capability | Is the needed capability missing? | Errors despite good conditions; a skill not present | Development (HEDP) |
| Connection | Can existing capabilities work together? | Duplicated work, missed handoffs, expertise out of reach | Capability Interconnection |
| Decision | Are decisions clear, timely, and owned? | Waiting for approval, reversed decisions, unclear ownership | Decision audit; [HEEn](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-enablement) where rights or information are missing |
| Enablement | Do people have the access, authority, information, and conditions to act? | Capable people blocked at the point of need | [HEEn](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-enablement) |
| Flow | Do work, decisions, information, and resources move without avoidable delay? | Queues, handoff delay, late inputs, interface friction | EES Fix (interfaces and flow); [3C](https://github.com/titumozammel/3c-strategic-approach) for context |
| Control | Are controls proportionate and effective? | Delay or dependency from controls; or no feedback at all | EES Control |
| Learning | Does execution experience inform a decision to change or deliberately keep an arrangement? | The same failure recurs; reviews produce no recorded decision | EES Adapt |

**Crosswalk to the Core’s seven diagnostic conditions [P].** The eight domains are a different cut from the Core’s provisional vocabulary (Human Energy Gap, Capability Gap, Capability Connection Gap, Enablement Gap, Execution Deficit, Drain Condition, Artificial Constraint Condition). They are not one-to-one: Demand may prompt investigation of a Drain Condition (legitimate demand is not itself drain); Capability to a Capability Gap; Connection to a Capability Connection Gap; Enablement to an Enablement Gap; and Decision partly to an Enablement Gap (when authority, information, or decision rights are the cause) and otherwise to the Core’s decision audit, since the Core records Decision Process Quality as a conversion condition without a separate gap. Flow, Control, and Learning have no separate Core condition, and a recurring restriction without a valid purpose may be recorded in the Core as an Artificial Constraint Condition. A Human Energy Gap is not an EES domain; EES defers to [HEMS](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-economics) for it. The Core’s Execution Deficit is the trigger that EES takes as its entry. The crosswalk is a proposal to test, not an established mapping.

### <a id="heart-head-hand-flow"></a>The HEART × HEAD × HAND × FLOW lens

**[P]** EES uses four words as a conceptual diagnostic lens: **HEART** (Human Energy and the human conditions affecting execution), **HEAD** (direction and clarity: strategy, priorities, decisions, roles, logic, alignment, shared understanding), **HAND** (execution capability: skills, accountability, discipline, consistency, completion, operational capability, control), and **FLOW** (movement of work, decisions, information, resources, dependencies, and outcomes). HEART is a prompt in the lens, not a domain claim; Human Energy as a diagnostic gap belongs to HEMS. The “×” is notation for interdependence, **not arithmetic**: the lens implies no multiplication, no weighting, and no claim that a zero in one element zeroes the others. It is not a validated equation, and the lens overlaps with the eight domains. Whether it adds classification value beyond the domains is a test in the research agenda; if each case maps one-to-one onto the domains, retire the lens.

## Definitions and distinctions

These definitions follow the closed HEE Core reference for this revision. Where EES adds a term, it says so.

| Term | Working meaning | Practical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity | Work performed | Not progress |
| Progress | Movement toward an intended outcome | Not execution |
| Capability | What a person or organization is able to do (potential) | Not capacity |
| Capacity | The part of capability that can actually be applied in a given period, given the time, attention, and energy available | Access, authority, and information belong to Enablement, not to capacity |
| Application | Putting capability to work | Having capacity does not guarantee it is applied, or applied to the right work |
| Execution | The reliable translation of application into coordinated action and results | Application alone is not execution |
| Reliable execution (EES term) | Execution whose results are produced as intended, repeatedly | Not the same as one good result |
| Sustained Execution | Producing a result repeatedly without degrading future execution capacity | Not the same as Sustainable Execution Capacity |
| Execution Deficit | An outcome in which expected execution does not occur reliably | A trigger for inquiry, not a cause |
| Sustainable Execution Capacity (SEC) [P] | Continuing ability and readiness to execute effectively over time | Requires follow-up evidence; one good cycle does not establish SEC |
| Constraint domain (EES term) [P] | A broad area in which a limit on execution may lie | A diagnostic domain, not a stage |
| Control | Mechanisms that stabilize an improvement | Not an objective in itself |
| Correction and adaptation | Correction fixes a deviation; adaptation changes the system in response to learning or changed conditions | A deviation may need only correction |

Therefore: **Activity ≠ Progress ≠ Execution ≠ Sustained Execution**, **Capability ≠ Capacity ≠ Application ≠ Execution**, and **Sustained Execution ≠ Sustainable Execution Capacity**. These are related conditions, not interchangeable words. An earlier edition wrote “Capacity = Application”; this edition follows the Core, where capacity concerns how much capability can be applied and application is putting it to work.

## The Decision Point

*Quoted from the Core README’s The Decision Point (the canonical text, without its illustrative example). If the two differ, the Core README governs.*

Within HEE, consequential decisions along the pathway from capability to sustainable results should be explicit and reviewable. Evaluate them using four distinct definitions:

1. **Decision Enablement:** whether the necessary decision rights, information, authority, and access exist.
2. **Decision Process Quality:** how the decision was reached.
3. **Decision Quality:** whether the choice was appropriate given what could reasonably be known.
4. **Decision Outcome Quality:** what actually happened.

**Model boundary.** Decision Process Quality remains the core model’s conversion condition. These four definitions are complementary assessment lenses, not four additional conversion conditions or compulsory sequential stages.

Assess these separately. A good outcome does not prove that the process or choice was sound; a poor outcome does not automatically establish that either was unsound. Context shapes every decision: its objective, legitimate demand, available evidence, constraints, alternatives, uncertainty, timing, and consequences.

**The Two Checks.** Apply the Two Checks proportionately to decisions affecting capacity:

- **Justified?** Was the expenditure of capacity warranted?
- **Covered?** Can credible expected usable capacity meet legitimate next-cycle demand?

A justified expenditure can still leave future capacity Not covered. Where evidence is insufficient, record Unknown and specify the next evidence request or precautionary action.

**Investigation routes.** Choose the route that fits the question:

- **Diagnose** (the Diagnostic Approach): investigate symptoms, test possible causes, and select a useful intervention.
- **Establish:** gather the evidence needed to make an uncertain question decidable.
- **Verify** (the Audit Approach): compare practice with a stated standard using traceable evidence.

These routes can inform one another; they are not compulsory sequential stages.

**The compressed conversion pathway and evidence boundaries.** Capability → Application → Execution → Value → Sustainable Growth. This compressed pathway does not replace the core model’s Human Energy, Connection, Decision Process Quality, Organizational Enablement, capacity, or renewal conditions. The Decision Point operates throughout this intended pathway. It makes choices and their consequences inspectable; it does not guarantee progression or sustainable results.

Customer Experience outcomes provide important evidence of customer-facing value. Sustainable Growth requires examining CX alongside employee experience, operating results, future-capacity coverage, and wider value consequences over time. Good CX alone does not establish that future delivery can be sustained.


**Applying the four definitions to execution.** Execution is where decisions meet consequences, so the four definitions separate what is easily blurred. *Decision Enablement:* did the people executing have the decision rights, information, authority, and access to act, or does someone else own the rule or the data? *Decision Process Quality:* how were the priorities, the fix, and the controls chosen, and were alternatives and safeguards considered? *Decision Quality:* given what could reasonably be known, was the choice appropriate? *Decision Outcome Quality:* what actually happened to the results and to next-cycle capacity? **Execution quality does not prove decision quality:** a poor decision can be executed efficiently, and a sound one can fail in execution. EES makes execution evidence available to later decisions; it does not replace decision-quality management.

**Investigation routes in the cycle.** The routes are not stages and can inform one another. In Find, **Diagnose** tests candidate causes and **Establish** gathers the evidence that makes an uncertain question decidable. In Control, **Verify** compares practice with a stated standard using traceable evidence. In Adapt, a review may use any of the three.

## What EES contributes

EES proposes one specific discipline: treat execution as a system that can be diagnosed across eight domains rather than assumed to be a matter of effort, fix the constraint that matters most rather than the most visible, keep the gain with proportionate control, make learning change something, and check next-cycle capacity before calling a result sustainable. Its intended contribution is a more inspectable path from an Execution Deficit to a fix, a control, and a next-cycle check, not the discovery that execution can be improved, or that constraints, learning, and control matter. **HEEn enables application; EES manages execution.** Neither requires the other.

Whether this discipline improves diagnosis, reliability, or outcomes beyond credible alternatives remains an empirical question. A new vocabulary or a successful example is not sufficient evidence of added value.

## One case from question to review

**All teams, quantities, thresholds, periods, and results below are invented [Illustrative]. This is neither an AKG case nor evidence for EES.**

A finance team’s month-end close has finished on working day 9 against a target of day 6 for four months. Leaders propose hiring two accountants.

**Observation, without a cause.** Over four closes, the close ends on day 9, 9, 10, and 9. In a sample of 40 delayed tasks, 35% waited for inputs from another team that arrived after day 4 (median two days late); 25% waited for one approver; 20% were reworked after late entries; the rest were ordinary effort. The shares are from the team’s own tally and are *estimated*.

**Find.** The evidence held for each domain:

| Domain | What the evidence holds | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connection | The sales and finance teams use different cut-off dates; no agreed interface | Estimated (tally); a candidate |
| Flow | Inputs arrive late and in batches; rework follows | Estimated; linked to Connection |
| Decision | One approver; waits lengthen when she is absent (two absences) | Measured in the sample |
| Capability | No sign of missing skill; errors cluster after late entries | Estimated; weak |
| Control | Approval limit set years ago; purpose and loss history **Not recorded** | **Unknown** |

**Priority (ECI).** The late inputs account for the most waiting, so the Connection and Flow condition is recorded as the priority, with Decision as secondary. The priority is a judgment on estimated evidence and is revisable. The approval limit stays untouched until its purpose is known.

**Alternatives still open.** The shares may be a tally artefact; the late inputs may be caused by an upstream system rather than by cut-off dates; the approval may protect against a real risk.

**Fix, with a stop rule.** Agree a cut-off date and a standing handoff with the sales team (the smallest reversible step), and add named approver cover for absences. The stop rule, written beforehand: if lateness does not fall by a pre-set share within two closes, or accrual errors rise, review the diagnosis.

**Control.** Track the day the close finishes, the number of late inputs, rework items, and approval waiting, each *measured* or *estimated*, reviewed at each close by a named owner. No extra sign-off is added, because a new check would reintroduce the waiting.

**Illustrative result.** The close finishes on day 7 and then day 6. The second close needed about 14 hours of unplanned overtime (measured from timesheets). The close finished earlier after the combined changes. Their separate contributions remain uncertain; demand variation, overtime, and other changes must also be examined. The result does not show that the gain is sustainable.

**Adapt and Grow.** The review finds the approver wait is now the longest delay, so approval cover is made permanent and a second approver is trained (a change). The approval limit is deliberately kept, with a review date, until its purpose is established (a decision to keep). The team records what it learned, what changed, and what it kept and why.

**The two checks.** *Justified?* The available evidence favored testing coordination and approval arrangements before hiring; it did not establish that additional staffing would provide no benefit. The existing effort was **partly** justified. *Covered?* Next-cycle capacity is **Fragile**: the improved close fits the base case but not a quarter-end close with the same overtime. A credible contingency is named: a person, a scheduled slot, the authority to use it, and a trigger date. A day-6 result achieved with unplanned overtime is a result, not evidence of sustained execution.

**The four decision reviews.** The owner’s authority to set the cut-off and the sales team’s access to the handoff establish Decision Enablement. The sample, the alternatives, and the safeguards describe Decision Process Quality. The choice is judged against what was known before it began. Close days, late inputs, rework, and overtime describe Decision Outcome Quality. These assessments may differ.

**What the example does not show.** It does not show that the cycle produced a better outcome than another method, that the diagnosis was right, that the priority judgment would be reproduced by another assessor, or that the gain will hold. Those are claims to test.

## Working principles

**[H] Heuristics, not laws.** Each holds unless evidence, stakes, or urgency say otherwise.

1. **Activity is not execution,** and performance is not automatically sustainable execution.
2. **Diagnose before intervening;** ask which domain, not who should work harder.
3. **Fix what matters most,** not what is merely most visible.
4. **Test the purpose of a rule or control before changing it,** and keep safeguards unless a replacement is shown.
5. **Capability is not enough:** it does not become execution without connection, enablement, decisions, and flow.
6. **Execution requires alignment** of priorities, ownership, and decision rights.
7. **Control stabilizes the gain;** more control is not better execution.
8. **Record learning separately from the decision to change or keep the system;** adaptation is part of execution, not a sign of failure.
9. **Prefer a reversible first step** with a stop rule written beforehand.
10. **Sustainable execution protects future capacity:** check the next cycle, not only the result.

## Sustainability, capacity, and value

**Sustainable Execution Capacity (SEC)** is the organization’s continuing ability and readiness to execute effectively over time. EES asks: **what did today’s execution do to tomorrow’s ability to execute?** Execution can consume Human Energy, capability, attention, relationships, flexibility, trust, learning capacity, and financial capacity. A result can be achieved while weakening the organization’s ability to reproduce it, so a result alone does not establish sustained execution.

**The sustainability test, with the Two Checks [H].** The EES test is *did today’s execution strengthen or unnecessarily weaken tomorrow’s execution capacity?* Apply it with the Core’s Two Checks. *Justified?* Was the capacity spent warranted (yes, partly, no)? *Covered?* Over a stated next-cycle horizon, does credible expected usable capacity meet legitimate demand? The evidence states are **Covered**, **Fragile**, **Not covered**, and **Unknown**. For **Fragile**, require a credible contingency (a named person, a scheduled slot, the resource or authority, and a trigger date); if any is missing, act as if Not covered. For **Unknown**, establish the missing evidence by a stated date, and if it passes without decisive evidence, act as if Not covered for the decision. See [OSF’s release and coverage section](https://github.com/titumozammel/operational-silence-framework#justified-expenditure-release-and-future-coverage) and the Core’s [one-page card](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-economics#justified-spending-or-future-shortfall-one-page-card-hp). A justified result can still leave a future shortfall; the two checks are read together.

**Value.** The proposed pathway is: reliable execution → learning → adaptation → stronger execution capability → SEC → sustainable business value **[P]**. EES contributes to the front of this pathway and guarantees no later step. Business value also depends on strategy, markets, customers, competition, economics, technology, regulation, and other external conditions. Good execution cannot indefinitely compensate for bad strategy.

**Growth and complexity.** Growth commonly adds people, systems, decisions, interfaces, dependencies, handoffs, rules, and exceptions, and with them coordination demand and friction. The conceptual risk is that execution demand outgrows execution capability. This is a hypothesis to test, not a law, and growth is not the problem.

**Experience and growth [P].** Execution conditions may change employee workload, clarity, rework, and autonomy, and may change what capacity can be applied and what reaches customers. Each link needs its own evidence, and a change at one does not establish a change at the next. Employee experience and customer experience are treated as evidence of operating conditions, not as assumed outcomes. Sustainable growth requires examining customer experience alongside employee experience, operating results, and future-capacity coverage over time; good customer experience alone does not establish that delivery can be sustained.

| Topic | What EES may help examine | What EES does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employee Experience | Execution conditions such as workload, clarity, coordination, rework, decision friction, and predictability | That experience will improve; EES does not own employee experience |
| Customer Experience | Execution contributors such as reliability, responsiveness, quality, consistency, resolution, and continuity | That a negative CX result proves internal execution failure; external conditions, product design, expectations, and strategy also matter |
| SLA and XLA | Whether execution is reliable enough to sustain service and experience commitments | That a missed commitment is an execution problem; EES does not replace SLA or XLA management |
| Human Energy | Execution conditions that consume or restrict capacity | That Human Energy has recovered or is determined by workflow alone; HEMS manages Human Energy |

## Boundaries and architecture

HEE is the provisional capacity lens informed by economic questions. **OEOS** is the proposed operating architecture; **HEMS**, **HEA**, **HERF**, and **HEDP** are Human Energy and capability-management components documented within the Core. These are proposed constructs, not validated systems. EES is an execution-management framework: the layer through which intended work becomes reliable, adaptive execution. It does not replace the mechanisms below, and it is not a mandatory stage after them.

| Related element | Its role | EES boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HEE Core | The capacity lens, diagnostic vocabulary, Two Checks, and research tests | The Core governs shared terms. It routes an Execution Deficit to Stabilize, Execute, Learn, and Adapt; EES gives that route a fuller structure (Find, Fix, Control, Adapt, Grow), and the two are not competing models |
| HEMS / HEA / HERF | Manage, audit, and recover Human Energy | EES may identify a Human Energy condition that materially affects execution; it does not take ownership. *HEMS manages the Human Energy condition; EES manages the execution consequence and response* |
| HEEn | Enabling existing capability to be applied | **HEEn enables application; EES manages execution.** Where the problem is access, authority, or information at the point of need, route to HEEn; a fix may involve both |
| OSF | Whether recurring operational demand should exist, and what happens to the capacity released | OSF reduces unnecessary demand entering the system; EES manages the reliability of the work that remains. Not every execution problem is a demand problem |
| 3C | Where to look: Source, Dependency, or Constraint | 3C directs contextual attention; EES manages the execution response. A signal is not a diagnosis |
| 5R | What to do with a recurring demand | 5R determines the disposition (remove, reduce, replace, re-engineer, or retain); EES manages how necessary work executes. A 5R change may reduce the demand entering execution. Where a 5R change is itself a Fix, record one owner and one review date for the gain, following the Core’s protection protocol for released capacity and EES Control for the stabilized improvement |
| HEDP | Develops genuinely missing capability | EES cannot supply a skill that is absent; a Capability finding routes to development |
| Capability Interconnection | Connects distributed capability | EES may identify a Connection problem; it does not itself connect capability |
| Decision-quality management | Improving how decisions are made | EES provides execution evidence for later decisions; it does not replace the discipline |
| Other disciplines | Strategy, operational management, project management, process improvement, Theory of Constraints, root cause analysis, organizational learning | EES integrates with their outputs where they materially affect execution |

<a id="which-tool-first"></a>**Which tool first [H].** If you are already reading EES, this routes you to a sibling framework. Unnecessary recurring demand: [OSF](https://github.com/titumozammel/operational-silence-framework) and [5R](https://github.com/titumozammel/5r-cascade-framework). Unsure where to look: [3C](https://github.com/titumozammel/3c-strategic-approach). Capable people blocked at the point of need: [HEEn](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-enablement). Depleted Human Energy: HEMS and HERF. A missing skill: HEDP. Unreliable execution with several interacting causes, or improvements that do not hold: EES.

**EES does not assume** that every performance problem is an execution problem, that every constraint should be removed, that faster is always better, that more activity creates more value, that capability automatically becomes capacity or execution, that every deviation requires intervention, that every recurring activity should be eliminated, that every problem needs more resources, that more control produces better execution, that every customer problem is internally caused, that every failure has one cause, that every solution should be standardized, or that short-term performance equals sustainable performance. Markets, regulation, engineering, strategy formulation, and specialist disciplines may need other explanations or experts.

## Theory and contribution

*For readers who want the positioning argument; owners can skip to the research agenda.* EES does not claim originality for systems thinking, quality management, continuous improvement, organizational learning, constraint management, execution as a discipline, strategy execution, feedback, control, adaptation, root-cause analysis, or iterative improvement. Each has established antecedents, and the proposed contribution is how they are integrated around reliable execution, learning, adaptation, and a next-cycle capacity check. EES is informed by established traditions **[E/A]**:

- **Systems thinking and quality.** W. Edwards Deming’s systems-oriented approach to quality, variation, management, and learning, and the plan-do-check-act cycle associated with Shewhart and Deming, are the nearest antecedents of a repeating improvement cycle. *Continuous improvement asks* how a process can be improved over cycles. *EES keeps that concern* and *explicitly includes* a diagnosis across eight domains, a priority step, and a next-cycle capacity check. DMAIC (define, measure, analyze, improve, control) also contains a control step; EES’s Control is not distinct in that respect.
- **Organizational learning.** Peter Senge’s work on learning organizations emphasizes systems thinking, shared understanding, and capability. *That literature asks* how organizations learn. *EES connects learning to execution* by requiring that experience change something, and it does not claim that its learning step is distinct from that tradition.
- **Execution as a discipline.** Larry Bossidy and Ram Charan treat execution as a distinct management discipline: many strategy failures arise because intentions are not translated into results. *EES’s premise* that capable, well-resourced organizations can still execute unreliably is consistent with it. EES’s addition is to ask what execution consumes, preserves, or strengthens in capacity terms.
- **Constraint management.** Eliyahu Goldratt’s Theory of Constraints holds that system performance is limited by constraints and that improvement should focus on them. *TOC asks* what limits the system. *EES keeps that question* and *explicitly includes* seven further domains beyond conventional bottleneck logic; it does not claim superiority over or replacement of TOC.
- **Strategy execution.** Lawrence Hrebiniak addresses the organizational conditions required to make strategy work: structure, coordination, information, controls, accountability, and change. EES is complementary; its narrower addition is the explicit link from reliable execution through learning and adaptation to future capacity.

The syntheses are the author’s reading of these traditions **[A]**, not a literature review. “Explicitly includes” describes EES’s content; whether those elements are absent from the established approaches is what the literature-positioning test is for.

The proposed contribution **[P]** is narrow: an integration of an eight-domain diagnosis, a priority judgment, proportionate control, a learning-to-adaptation record, and a next-cycle capacity check inside the HEE vocabulary. Whether this adds decision value beyond plan-do-check-act, DMAIC, and the Theory of Constraints must be tested against credible alternatives with comparable evidence and effort. The eight domains may overlap substantially with each other and with established ideas; EES does not presently show that they are distinct. If the tests below find that they collapse, narrow EES to a set of diagnostic prompts.

| Approach | Primary starting point | Proposed EES distinction (to be tested, not established) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan-do-check-act | An improvement cycle | Adds an eight-domain diagnosis, a priority step, and a next-cycle capacity check |
| DMAIC and Six Sigma | Process variation and defects | Concerns the execution system and not only a process; the Control step overlaps |
| Theory of Constraints | The system constraint | The constraint is one of eight domains, with a legitimacy test before change |
| Root cause analysis | A cause | Treats cause as one of several domains; adds control and learning |
| Organizational learning | How organizations learn | Requires that learning change something and links it to execution reliability |
| Execution as a discipline | Disciplined follow-through | Adds a capacity view: what execution consumes and leaves for the next cycle |
| Strategy execution | Conditions that make strategy work | Concerns the execution system within a given strategy |
| Project management | Delivering a defined scope | Concerns recurring execution reliability, not a single project |
| OSF, 3C, 5R, HEEn | Demand, context, disposition, enablement | Manage the execution that remains after demand and conditions are addressed |

**Research versus practice.** A heuristic may help structure inquiry without being a validated causal model. Retain, merge, narrow, or retire labels when evidence does not support their intended role.

MIT Center for Advanced Engineering Study. · Goldratt, E. M., & Cox, J. (1984). *The Goal: A Process of Ongoing Improvement*. North River Press. · Hrebiniak, L. G. ## Research agenda and tests

The following are proposed EES study questions, **not newly registered Core propositions**. References to P10, P11, P14, and P15 indicate links to existing Core tests, not a claim that these studies have occurred or been preregistered.

**Which test to run first.** Start with *domain classification reliability* using the [coding sheet](#domain-coding-sheet-and-first-reliability-check), because claims that depend on domain labels need reliable classification. Comprehension and reader-utility studies can proceed separately, and the prompts may prove useful even if some labels overlap. The *literature-positioning* test is desk-based and can run in parallel. Run the *priority* and *cycle value* tests only after reliability is acceptable.

| Test | Core proposition | Design and measures | Evidence supporting a bounded claim | Evidence weakening or narrowing it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Domain classification reliability | None: EES-specific (parallels P11) | The investigator defines the parts of each packet before rating, so raters classify the same units (if raters segment packets themselves, report segmentation agreement separately). Independent assessors record every applicable domain for each part, with a rationale and a primary label where one applies, before discussion; allow Unknown and Outside EES; report raw and chance-corrected agreement on the primary label and, separately, agreement on the set of domains named (exact match and mean Jaccard similarity), with intervals; pre-register thresholds | Agreement exceeds a pre-specified threshold | Adequately documented packets cannot be classified reliably; revise definitions or reduce EES to prompts |
| Distinctness of the domains | None: EES-specific | For the same packets, have each independent assessor state, **before naming any domain,** the evidence each of the eight domains would prompt them to seek (a within-assessor comparison); code each request into a pre-specified evidence-type list; for each packet compute the Jaccard similarity between the sets of evidence types prompted by each pair of domains, report the mean and range, and report agreement between assessors on a three-way judgment (*distinct / overlapping / indistinguishable*) for each pair; pre-specify and justify the thresholds before analysis, setting them from pilot data | The domains prompt meaningfully different evidence requests | The requests converge or labels are used interchangeably; merge domains |
| Crosswalk to the Core’s seven conditions | None: EES-specific (relates to P11) | Have assessors code the same packets in both vocabularies, allowing multiple mappings; report the cross-tabulation, and test whether the proposed correspondences are consistent across assessors, interpretable, and useful for routing | The correspondences are consistent and interpretable at a pre-specified level, and routing by them is no worse | The correspondences are inconsistent or uninterpretable; withdraw the crosswalk and keep each vocabulary separate |
| Priority (ECI) agreement | None: EES-specific | Independent assessors given the same packet name the constraint that matters most, or Unknown, before discussion; report chance-corrected agreement; compare with a pre-specified comparator ranking (the Theory of Constraints focusing logic, and a simple impact-and-effort ranking). A later field stage tests whether fixing the named constraint moves a pre-named outcome more than fixing other candidates, using matched or staggered comparison | Agreement exceeds a pre-specified threshold, and the named constraint outperforms alternatives | Assessors disagree, or the named constraint does no better than the comparators; treat ECI as a discussion prompt, not a method |
| Cycle value | None: EES-specific (relates to P10 Stage 0) | Compare EES-guided diagnoses and plans with a named, versioned comparator using equal information, attention, and expertise: **primary:** a plan-do-check-act or DMAIC worksheet; **secondary:** the Theory of Constraints focusing steps. Blinded experts rate evidence fit, risk to necessary safeguards, and whether the plan covers control, learning, and next-cycle capacity | Consistent, justified differences in plans or evidence requests | No meaningful difference; narrow novelty to the domain prompts and the next-cycle check |
| Control proportionality | None: EES-specific | In comparable workflows, compare execution reliability and the burden of control (hours on indicators, reviews, and approvals; delay attributed to controls) across control designs; pre-set follow-up intervals | A design shows equal or better reliability at lower control burden | No difference; withdraw the claim that proportionality is a distinct property of EES Control |
| Learning to adaptation | None: EES-specific | After execution reviews, record the share of lessons that lead to a recorded decision (to change or to deliberately keep an arrangement) and the share that lead to a change of a pre-specified type within a pre-set interval, and whether the same failure recurs, against matched teams without a structured review | Lessons convert to documented decisions (to change or to deliberately keep an arrangement), and where change is made the specified outcome improves | Reviews produce no documented learning or reasoned decision; or, where change is warranted and implemented, an adequate comparison finds no meaningful improvement in the specified outcome. Narrow the corresponding claim |
| Next-cycle coverage | None: EES-specific (relates to P14) | Classify cases on the Two Checks before outcomes; compare the Justified? and Covered? judgments at result time with a results-only assessment for predicting a next-cycle shortfall or degradation | The sustainability test adds predictive value | No added value; narrow the sustainability test to a prompt |
| Four-part lens | None: EES-specific | Code the same packets with HEART, HEAD, HAND, FLOW and with the eight domains; test whether the lens classifies cases the domains do not | The lens adds classification value | Each case maps one-to-one onto the domains; retire the lens |
| Literature positioning | None: EES-specific (unowned; parallels the P12 synthesis) | A documented, protocol-based review of systems thinking and quality, organizational learning, constraint management, and strategy execution; two independent coders map each EES element and question to the nearest established construct, with the search strategy and disagreements recorded | Documented gaps: at least one EES element (the eight-domain diagnosis, the next-cycle capacity check, the learning-to-adaptation record) has no close established equivalent | Two or more independent coders map every EES element onto an existing construct with agreement above a pre-specified threshold; narrow EES to a diagnostic vocabulary |
| Reader utility | P15 | Representative role-based tasks using this document versus a credible comparator; predefine completion-time and blinded quality criteria | Faster answers without unacceptable quality loss | Speed worsens or important answers cannot be found; revise routing |

| If a fair test finds | Then |
| --- | --- |
| Assessors cannot classify domains reliably | EES is a set of prompts, not a classification; do not report domain labels as findings |
| The domains cannot be told apart | Merge them; withdraw the claim that there are eight distinct domains |
| Assessors cannot agree on which constraint matters most | ECI is a discussion prompt, not a method; do not report a priority as a finding |
| EES-guided plans do not differ from plan-do-check-act, DMAIC, or Theory of Constraints plans | EES is a vocabulary and checklist on top of existing methods rather than a new approach |
| Control designs do not differ in reliability or burden | Withdraw the claim that proportionate control is distinctive |
| Reviews lead to no recorded decisions or recurrence is unchanged | Withdraw the claim that Adapt closes the loop |
| The Two Checks add nothing to a results-only assessment | Narrow the sustainability test to a prompt |
| The four-part lens maps one-to-one onto the domains | Retire the lens |

A sufficiently precise null or contrary result after a fair test should narrow or reject the specified claim. Do not rescue a failed claim by changing definitions after seeing outcomes. See also the Core’s [falsification summary](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-economics#falsification-summary-where-to-start-p).

### Domain coding sheet and first reliability check

This is a starter instrument **[P]** for the domain classification test. All packets are **[Illustrative]** and show how the check works; they are not findings. Eight packets are enough to train raters and pilot the sheet, not to estimate reliability, which needs a sample sized in advance.

**Rating sheet (one row per part and rater).** Record: packet ID and part; rater; every applicable domain (*Demand / Capability / Connection / Decision / Enablement / Flow / Control / Learning, singly or in combination, or Unknown / Outside EES*) with a one-line rationale and a primary label where one applies; the constraint the rater judges matters most; the evidence cited; the legitimacy of any rule or control involved (*legitimate / unnecessary / Unknown*); and what further evidence the rater would request. Raters work independently and do not discuss packets until agreement has been computed.

**Provisional coding rule [P].** Code every domain that the packet’s evidence directly supports, not those the rater finds plausible; name a primary domain only if the evidence ranks them. Where a packet’s parts point to different domains, the investigator defines the parts before rating. Code *Unknown* when the evidence supports none, and *Outside EES* when the primary problem lies in strategy, markets, or other external conditions. Revise the rule if raters cannot apply it, and version every revision.

| ID | Case packet | What it is designed to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | Two teams use different customer identifiers, so work is re-keyed at every handoff | Connection, with Flow |
| E2 | Purchase orders wait for one approver; waits lengthen when she is absent | Decision, possibly Enablement (decision rights) |
| E3 | A new system went live; staff were not trained and errors rose | Capability |
| E4 | The same defect recurs each quarter; reviews list lessons but record no decision on them, and no one owns them | Learning, possibly Control |
| E5 | A weekly report goes to fourteen managers; no use is on record and recipients were not asked | Demand, with Unknown (route to OSF and 5R) |
| E6 | Three overlapping checks add two days to delivery; the purpose of two is not recorded | Control, with Unknown legitimacy |
| E7 | A capable team gets weak results; priorities conflict and ownership is unclear | Decision, with a candidate second domain |
| E8 | Orders fall because a major customer left the market | Outside EES |

**Worked coded case [Illustrative].** A report is late four months running. The investigator predefines two parts from a 40-task sample: *Part A*, waiting for inputs from another team; *Part B*, waiting for one approver. *Rater A:* Part A, Connection (primary) and Flow; Part B, Decision. *Rater B:* Part A, Flow (primary) and Connection; Part B, Decision (primary) and Enablement (the approver may lack delegated authority). *Agreement, computed before discussion:* primary labels agree on Part B and differ on Part A (1 of 2); the sets are identical on Part A and differ on Part B (exact match 1 of 2; Jaccard 1.0 and 0.5, mean 0.75). *Reconciliation, after agreement is computed:* on Part A the evidence does not rank Connection above Flow, so record both without a primary; on Part B the Enablement reading depends on whether delegation exists, which the packet does not state, so record Decision with Enablement as a candidate that needs the owner’s evidence. Agreement on the primary label and on the set are different questions, and a rater pair can do well on one and poorly on the other.

**First reliability procedure [P].** (1) Pre-specify the threshold, the sample size, and the coefficients before rating. (2) Train raters on three packets; do not reuse them for the estimate. (3) Rate fresh packets independently, giving every rater the same evidence and the same predefined parts, with *Unknown* and *Outside EES* allowed and counted as categories. (4) Report raw agreement, a chance-corrected coefficient (for two raters, Cohen’s kappa), and an interval for the primary label of each part, and separately the agreement on the set of domains named. (5) Only then discuss disagreements, revise the rule, and record the version. (6) Re-rate a fresh set under the revised rule. (7) Compare the evidence and the priority each rater would name, because raters can agree on a label and still ask for different evidence. The arithmetic of raw agreement, kappa, and an interval is shown in the [worked reliability example in OSF](https://github.com/titumozammel/operational-silence-framework#necessity-coding-pack-and-first-reliability-check); the numbers there are illustrative and carry no evidence about EES.

### A short learner exercise
